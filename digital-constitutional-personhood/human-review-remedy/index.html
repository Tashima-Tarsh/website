Human Review and Remedy in Digital Systems

A serious digital decision requires more than a generated result. The citizen must be able to understand, challenge and correct the record through an accountable human path.

Minimum remedy architecture

  • notice of the decision and its practical effect;
  • access to the relevant record or reason;
  • correction where the record is wrong;
  • human review by an identified authority;
  • preservation of evidence for appeal or audit.

Why Article 12 matters

Human review is not a customer-service preference when public power is involved. It is the route through which responsibility becomes visible and remedy becomes possible.

Limits

This page is a framework note. It does not assert that any particular institution has denied review without a source record.

Why human review cannot be optional

Digital systems that affect citizens' access to services, benefits, records, liberty or dignity are not neutral technical processes; they are exercises of public power mediated by software. When a digital system produces a result — a denial, a rejection, a risk label, a benefit suspension, a grievance closure — that result has the same constitutional character as a decision made by a human official. The citizen's right to challenge that decision, to understand the basis for it, and to have a human authority review and correct it, is not a technological preference. It is a constitutional requirement that flows from Articles 14, 19 and 21 of the Indian Constitution as interpreted by decades of Supreme Court jurisprudence on administrative fairness, procedural due process and the right to personal liberty.

The DISHA research finds that automated systems in public governance are frequently designed with disposal efficiency in mind rather than remedy adequacy. A system that generates 10,000 grievance closures per month may be meeting its processing targets while producing very few genuine resolutions. Human review is the mechanism through which the gap between disposal and resolution is identified and addressed. Without it, the system optimises for its own outputs rather than for the citizen's constitutional outcomes.

What meaningful human review requires

Human review that is genuinely constitutional in character requires more than the existence of a nominal appellate mechanism. It requires that the reviewer have actual authority to override the original decision; that the reviewer have access to the full record on which the original decision was based, including any algorithmic inputs or model outputs; that the reviewer provide a reasoned response that the citizen can understand; that the response be delivered within a defined and constitutionally adequate time period; and that, if the review itself is adverse, a further appeal path be available. Where any of these elements is absent — where the reviewer lacks override authority, or where the review consists only of confirming the original automated result without independent examination — the human review requirement has not been met in substance, regardless of whether it has been met in form.

Remedy: beyond acknowledgement

The remedy obligation in the Digital Constitutional Personhood framework requires public authorities to do more than acknowledge that a citizen has raised a concern. Acknowledgement is the beginning of the accountability process, not its end. A meaningful remedy returns the citizen to the position they should have been in had the failure not occurred: the benefit is restored, the record is corrected, the identity credential is reinstated, the misidentification is resolved. Where full restoration is not possible — because harm has already been suffered, because a time-sensitive entitlement has been irretrievably lost, or because the system failure is structural rather than individual — the remedy obligation requires disclosure of what happened, explanation of why restoration is not possible, and a commitment to systemic correction to prevent the same failure for other citizens.

The DISHA archive's remedy record

When the DISHA intelligence archive holds a case file involving a denial of human review or remedy, the evidence record will include: the original decision or system output; the citizen's request for review; the official response, if any; the time taken to respond; whether the response addressed the substantive question or merely restated the original result; and whether the matter was ultimately resolved or remains open. Cases in which the archive identifies a complete absence of human review for a consequential automated decision are recorded as unresolved accountability questions under the DISHA claim-to-source system, and may form part of the evidence base for public-interest litigation including the Supreme Court petitions W.P.(Crl.) 163/2026 and W.P.(Crl.) 394/2025.
