Thesis

The money is the symptom. The data is the crime scene.

The petitioner's central argument is direct: a digital-fraud system cannot be solved only by freezing accounts after money leaves. If identity data, KYC files, call signals, location patterns, device permissions, and behavioural metadata have already escaped into foreign or criminal infrastructure, the same citizens can be targeted again and again.

You cannot SOP your way out of a data breach that has already happened. Follow the data, or the citizen remains invisible.

That is why this case treats data recovery, data destruction, victim notification, extradition of alleged architects, and audit of the surveillance layer as root-cause remedies, not side issues.

Root cause

The alleged three-layer data architecture.

  1. Android permission exploitation: loan apps allegedly demanded contacts, precise location, call logs, accounts, and device access unrelated to lending.
  2. Shell-NBFC KYC layer: formal finance surfaces allegedly collected Aadhaar, PAN, bank, face, address, and phone records under the appearance of compliance.
  3. AdTech SDK surveillance layer: the uninvestigated layer alleged to explain live victim profiling, behavioural timing, and high-pressure targeting.

The case does not claim every listed technology company ran the fraud. The sharper claim is that documented surveillance technologies and SDK behaviour should have been investigated under Indian law where they may have enabled profiling.

AdTech layer

The second body of the crime.

  • InMobi: FTC settlement, June 2016, federal court docket No. 3:16-cv-03474, involving WiFi/BSSID-based geolocation concerns, child-privacy findings, a USD 950,000 payment, and a 20-year privacy program.
  • SilverPush: FTC warning letters, March 2016, concerning ultrasonic audio-beacon technology and cross-device tracking through device microphones.
  • MeitY question: the petition asks whether India opened any inquiry under IT Act Section 43A after these public foreign regulatory records.

DISHA Intelligence Finding

The Weaponization of Identity — India 2012–2026

Condensed forensic intelligence record derived from the monograph The Weaponization of Identity: A Constitutional Demand for Digital Personhood in India (Ch. 1–11). The record traces Indian citizen data from collection through aggregation, export, and weaponization. Facts, entities, figures, dates, and tables are preserved below. All source attributions follow the primary source document. This finding is filed as supplementary intelligence to Case 02.

Thesis

Data Pipeline: Collection → Aggregation → Export → Weaponization

This intelligence record traces Indian citizen data across a twelve-year span (2012–2026). The central demand is constitutional recognition of Digital Personhood as a Fundamental Right. Primary sources cited include NCRB, CERT-In, MHA/I4C, RBI, ED, CBI, Supreme Court, High Courts, Citizen Lab, CloudSEK, Cyble, FTC, EDPS, Parliamentary Standing Committees, published FIRs, chargesheets, and RBI orders.

Finding 01 / Ch. 1

Antivirus and Tech-Support Scam Era (2010–2015): Origins

The fraud ecosystem emerged around 2008–2010 in BPO hubs — Noida, Gurugram, Kolkata, Ahmedabad, and Bengaluru — reusing existing BPO infrastructure including English-speaking call staff, VoIP lines, and international calling capacity. Operators spoofed VoIP caller-ID for US, UK, Canadian, and Australian numbers, impersonated Microsoft, Norton, McAfee, and "Windows Technical Dept," and induced victims to open Event Viewer (where benign logs were presented as proof of infection). Remote-access tools — AnyDesk, TeamViewer, LogMeIn — were then installed. Revenue streams included fake removal fees of ₹8,000–₹50,000, recurring fake annual maintenance contracts, banking credential theft, ransomware installation, and sale of harvested data.

This coincided with rising Aadhaar adoption, the Jio 2016 rollout, and the influx of Chinese fintech capital.

Finding 02 / Ch. 2

AdTech and SDK Surveillance Economy: Data Collection

SilverPush Technologies (founded 2012, Gurugram) deployed ultrasonic cross-device tracking — television advertisements embedded inaudible beacons above 18 kHz while apps carrying the SilverPush SDK listened via the device microphone, logging advertisement identity, broadcast time, channel, device ID, and location, then linking TV-viewing behaviour to mobile identity. Users were unaware; microphone permission appeared as a generic host-application request. The FTC issued warning letters in March 2016 to 12 app developers. The University of Brunswick documented 234 Android apps carrying the SDK, including popular Indian utility apps. Indian regulatory response: none.

InMobi (founded 2007, Bengaluru) was sanctioned by the FTC in June 2016 for tracking the location of hundreds of millions of users — including children — without consent, using Wi-Fi scanning to bypass denied GPS permissions. The company's SDK was present in under-13 apps in violation of COPPA. Penalty: USD 4 million reduced to USD 950,000; mandatory privacy program; 20-year FTC oversight. Indian regulatory response: zero. The same SDK continued serving Indian users.

Finding 05 / Ch. 5

NBFC Fraud Economy: Predatory Finance

Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) operate under Companies Act registration without a banking licence; RBI regulation applies but with lighter requirements. The sector was attractive for fraud because of weaker beneficial-ownership disclosure, a minimum net-owned fund of ₹2 Cr (raised to ₹10 Cr only in 2021), lax "fit and proper" enforcement, permission to outsource entire operations to unregulated third parties, and the absence of app-lending regulation until 2022 — a four-year gap from 2018.

Four-entity model: (1) Chinese holding company (HK/Cayman/BVI) with no Indian filing and nominee directors; (2) Indian NBFC with nameplate directors; (3) Indian "tech company" running the app, KYC, disbursement, and collections — paid "tech fees" that represented actual profit, and outside RBI oversight; (4) recovery-agent company running harassment call centres, insulated from the NBFC.

Finding 07 / Ch. 7

Aadhaar: Unsecured Billion-Person Database

UIDAI was established in 2009. The Aadhaar database (CIDR) holds 12-digit identifiers linked to 10 fingerprints, 2 iris scans, and a photograph for each enrolled individual. By 2018: 1.2 billion enrolled — a dataset larger than the combined records of FBI, CIA, NSA, and European intelligence agencies.

Tribune investigation (3 January 2018, reporter Rachna Khaira): ₹500 paid to a WhatsApp seller yielded — within 10 minutes — portal access for full-text Aadhaar search. Searching by Aadhaar number returned name, father's name, address, date of birth, mobile number, photograph, and gender. Searching by mobile number returned the linked Aadhaar and demographic record. An additional ₹300 returned bank account number and IFSC. A further ₹500 yielded "Aadhaar printing software" capable of producing physical cards with real data and altered names, usable for SIM acquisition, hotel check-in, and KYC. The investigation verified this by searching the UIDAI Chairman's own record.

UIDAI response: Day 1 — "completely safe, no hacker can access CIDR." Day 2 — filed an FIR against the journalist under Aadhaar Act Sections 37 and 38, making no distinction between the data leaker and the reporter. UIDAI did not advise 1.2 billion enrolled individuals, freeze the API, commission an independent audit, or inform Parliament. Researcher Srinivas Kodali found 135+ government portals still exposing data six months later. The FIR was dropped in 2020, after its chilling effect on investigative journalism had operated.

Structural vulnerabilities identified: Weak API authentication logging with no alert for mass queries; unaudited operator credential security (millions of operators); BSNL/telecom gateway (2024 BSNL breach = 278 GB SIM/IMSI data → Aadhaar phone-verification pathway); 210+ misconfigured state portals; 50,000+ KUAs unverified; no citizen real-time authentication alert (Aadhaar used to open accounts, SIMs, and loans without notification to the citizen).

Finding 08 / Ch. 8

CoWIN: Mandatory Health Data Exposed

Co-WIN launched in January 2021 as India's national COVID vaccination backbone. By June 2023: approximately 950 million registrations, each linking Aadhaar to mobile number, vaccination centre, health record, family structure, and location. Registration was mandatory — no vaccine was available without it.

Legal insufficiency note: "No central-server breach" does not discharge government liability. The data controller (Government of India) remains responsible regardless of whether the vector was an intermediary. Zero of approximately 150 million affected individuals were notified. Data collection was mandatory, imposing a heightened duty of care. The breach constitutes ongoing harm while data circulates on criminal markets.

Finding 09 / Ch. 9

Finding 10 / Ch. 10

Digital Arrest: ₹2,140 Crore Psychological Fraud

Digital arrest is a low-technology, high-psychology fraud. Its effectiveness derives from the caller possessing real victim specifics: exact Aadhaar number, mobile number, full address including flat number, a family member's name and contact, bank name and approximate balance, employer and designation, and recent travel city and date. This data is sourced from the Aadhaar (2018+), ICMR 815-million-record breach (2023), CoWIN 150-million-record breach (2023), BSNL dataset (2024), insider-sold financial data, and criminal broker marketplaces.

Finding 11 / Ch. 11

Cambodia–Myanmar–China Triangle: Trafficking and Impunity

The fraud is executed from Myanmar (Myawaddy, Karen State), Cambodia (Sihanoukville), and Yunnan/Guangdong (China). Crimes are committed against Indian victims; perpetrators remain physically beyond Indian legal reach.

Myanmar: Post-2021 coup, Karen Border Guard Force and local militias lease land to Chinese syndicates operating compounds with dormitories and call centres. Workers are trafficked through fake Singapore and Thailand job advertisements. GASO estimates 100,000+ trafficked workers by 2023. MEA rescued 250+ Indians in 2023–24; peak estimates place 1,500–3,000 Indians in these compounds. Indian workers were recruited via Facebook, Instagram, and Telegram advertisements in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Kolkata, Delhi, and Chennai, offering ₹60,000–₹1.5 lakh per month for "data entry/marketing/customer service." CBI chargesheet of November 2024 named 11 Indian coordinators; transaction price per trafficked worker: USD 3,000–10,000; charges include human trafficking and PMLA. Conditions inside include physical beatings, resale between compounds, forced pornography, and ransom demands. Several Indian deaths have been reported (count unconfirmed). MEA maintains back-channel engagement with the Myanmar junta and militias; a tripartite Thailand–Myanmar framework was under negotiation as of 2026.

Cambodia: Sihanoukville casino infrastructure, repurposed after the 2019 gambling crackdown, hosts similar operations. Operation Chakra-III (CBI + Interpol, 2024) identified 47 Indians — mostly voluntary recruits subsequently trapped by document confiscation and debt bondage.

China layer: Triad-affiliated networks, with tacit tolerance from Yunnan and Guangdong provincial authorities, provide servers, VoIP infrastructure, AI voice changers, CRM systems, USDT routing, layered wire transfers, technical supervisors, and structural impunity. Loan-app syndicates and scam-compound syndicates share the same HK/Cayman shell infrastructure, Singapore and UAE banking, personnel, and — in documented cases — identical server IP addresses. The NBFC crackdown drove a capital pivot toward trafficked-worker call centres.

Cross-Cutting Constants

Structural Patterns Across All Eleven Findings

  • Accountability asymmetry: Foreign agencies investigate → Indian cooperation remains marginal → masterminds escape.
  • Recurring breach feedstock: Aadhaar (2018+), CoWIN (approximately 150 million records, 2023), ICMR (815 million records, 2023), BSNL (278 GB, 2024) → these datasets directly enable digital-arrest victim personalization.
  • Regulation lag: Harm consistently precedes regulatory response by 3–4 years (loan-app fraud from 2018; RBI guidelines only in 2022).
  • Jurisdiction gaps: No India–China extradition treaty; UAE MLAT latency 6–18 months; Myanmar and Cambodia servers physically inaccessible.
  • Constitutional demand: Digital Personhood as a Fundamental Right — the systemic remedy proposed across all eleven findings.
