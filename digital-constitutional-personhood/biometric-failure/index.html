Identity and dignity

Biometric Failure and Constitutional Visibility

The framework treats biometric identity as a representation of the citizen, not the citizen. A failed match, corrupted record or unavailable device should not become a final denial without explanation, correction and human review.

Where the harm occurs

Biometric failure can turn a living person into a failed record. The constitutional concern is not only technical accuracy; it is whether the person remains visible to the authority responsible for service, correction and remedy.

Record needed

  • what biometric or identity check failed;
  • who controlled the database or device;
  • what alternative proof was accepted or refused;
  • who reviewed the failure;
  • what correction or appeal path existed.

Limits

This page does not make a finding about any specific biometric programme. It defines the archive’s evidentiary questions for future records.

How biometric identity systems work in Indian governance

India’s biometric identity infrastructure is centred on the Aadhaar system, which links a unique identification number to fingerprint and iris biometric data collected from residents. The Aadhaar-linked biometric is used across a wide range of public and private functions: benefit delivery under schemes such as the Public Distribution System and MGNREGA, bank account opening, SIM card registration, digital signature services, income tax filing and government portal access. The scale of this integration means that a biometric failure — whether caused by poor scan quality, sensor malfunction, data-entry error, network outage, or a mismatch between the enrolled template and the current biometric reading — can block a citizen’s access to multiple essential services simultaneously. An elderly agricultural worker whose fingerprints are worn from manual labour, or a citizen whose iris scan fails on a malfunctioning device, may be unable to draw their food ration, receive their employment payment or access their bank account through any of the systems that depend on the same biometric credential.

Biometric failure as a constitutional event

The Digital Constitutional Personhood framework treats a biometric failure that results in a denial of essential services as a constitutional event, not merely a technical inconvenience. Under Article 21 of the Indian Constitution, the right to life includes the right to livelihood and the right to receive the services and entitlements that the State has committed to provide. When a biometric system failure prevents a citizen from accessing those entitlements, and when no alternative verification process is available or offered, the constitutional right to life is directly implicated. The framework does not assert that every biometric system failure constitutes a constitutional violation; it asserts that a failure that results in denial, without a functioning alternative, explanation or appeal, raises a constitutional accountability question that the responsible public authority must answer.

The alternative proof and human review obligation

One of the clearest accountability tests for biometric failure is whether the public authority responsible for the system has provided an alternative proof pathway. An authority that knows its biometric system is prone to failure — through technical limitations, poor infrastructure or inadequate maintenance — and that has not designed and implemented a robust alternative verification process has failed to meet a basic constitutional standard. The record needed in such cases includes: documentation of what the citizen presented as alternative identity proof; the official’s response to that alternative; whether a supervisor or reviewing authority was involved; and whether any written reason was given for the final denial. In the absence of alternative proof acceptance and human review, the denial is not a technical outcome; it is an administrative act whose constitutional validity is open to challenge.

Fraud and biometric exploitation

Biometric failure is not always caused by system malfunction. Digital arrest fraud and identity theft can involve deliberate exploitation of biometric and identity systems: fraudsters may use stolen biometric data to create false authentication events, or may use social engineering to access biometric-linked accounts during a digital arrest interaction. Where biometric credentials are compromised through fraud, the victim faces the additional burden of demonstrating that the authentication event was not authorised, which in turn requires access to the biometric system’s own logs. The DISHA archive’s accountability question in fraud-linked biometric cases is which authority holds those logs, whether they are available to the victim, and what correction and recovery process has been made accessible.
