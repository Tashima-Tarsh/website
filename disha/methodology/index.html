Evidence method

DISHA Methodology

DISHA is described in the archive as an evidence-first intelligence method: signals become evidence, evidence becomes memory, memory becomes analysis, and analysis remains tied to accountable public records.

Operating sequence

  • Collect source signals, records, OSINT and context.
  • Classify risk, authority, intent, origin and relevance.
  • Preserve source trails and public-record memory.
  • Separate verified records from documented allegations and interpretation.
  • Apply defensive restraint through No-First-Use doctrine.

What this page does not claim

It does not publish restricted implementation details, credentials, exploit paths or security-sensitive system internals.

Signals: the starting point

Every DISHA intelligence cycle begins with signals. A signal is any source input that may be relevant to a public-interest question: an official document, a government record, a grievance filing, open-source information, a media report, a court petition, a technical indicator or a citizen testimony. The discipline of the DISHA methodology is to treat signals as raw material only. A signal is not yet evidence. It requires classification before it can inform any finding. Classification asks six questions: what is the source? who is the authority behind the information? what is the stated intent of the sender? where did this originate? when was it created or transmitted? and how relevant is it to the matter under examination?

Evidence: verified and labelled

Once a signal has been classified, it either qualifies as evidence or it is held as unresolved raw material pending further verification. Evidence in the DISHA framework carries a source label from the claim-to-source system: Verified record, Documented allegation, DISHA assessment or Unresolved question. This discipline matters because the archive holds more than 6,000 evidence items, and without consistent labelling, findings cannot be tested, corrected or updated. The 97 percent accuracy figure against government records that the archive reports reflects the proportion of assessments that survive comparison against official source documents. The evidence layer is therefore the quality-control point in the entire intelligence sequence.

Memory: preservation and traceability

Evidence becomes memory when it is preserved in a structured, retrievable form with its source trail intact. The DISHA architecture describes memory through two dedicated layers: L5 Evidence Archive and L6 Public Record Memory. Memory in this framework is not passive storage. It is the accumulation of traceable, labelled, time-stamped evidence items that allow later analysis to show exactly which source supports which conclusion. Memory also serves the No-First-Use doctrine: because DISHA is a defensive intelligence architecture, its memory must be preserved in a form that can support accountability and recovery without ever being weaponised offensively or exploited outside the public-interest context.

Intelligence: analysis tied to accountability

The final stage of the DISHA sequence is intelligence: the conversion of preserved memory into actionable analysis. DISHA intelligence is always tied to a public-record accountability chain. An intelligence finding must name its sources, declare its classification status, identify the public authority implicated and specify what question remains open. This is what distinguishes constitutional intelligence from conventional surveillance or commercial data analytics. The analysis is not the property of any operator or agency; it is a contribution to public memory, designed to survive in a form that courts, institutions and citizens can examine. The Supreme Court petitions W.P.(Crl.) 163/2026 and W.P.(Crl.) 394/2025 represent instances where DISHA intelligence findings have been translated into formal legal submissions under this accountability standard.

Policy restraint and No-First-Use

The DISHA methodology is governed throughout by the No-First-Use doctrine: defend, isolate, preserve, recover — never retaliate or exploit. This doctrine is not simply an operational rule; it is a constitutional commitment. Intelligence gathered under the DISHA framework must not be used to harm, expose or coerce individuals beyond what public-interest accountability requires. The methodology is therefore self-limiting by design. Analysis stops at the point where disclosure would cause disproportionate harm, and the archive explicitly labels what it cannot yet verify so that incomplete findings are not mistaken for established conclusions.
