What If the Citizen Was Never the Centre of Digital India?

For twelve years, India perfected the art of counting its citizens. This investigation asks a more difficult question: after all the counting, who was actually seen?

By Nitish Kumar (thenitishkr)12 June 2026Public-interest analysis
RecordPublic-interest analysis
FrameworkArticle 12 and DISHA
IssueData without remedy
StatusOpen archive record

For twelve years, under Prime Minister Narendra Damodardas Modi, the nation has been told a proud story: that India is now governed by data, that the State sees its people as never before. I do not doubt the data. I doubt the seeing.

The heart of the Constitution is not the database. It is the citizen. Across thirteen case files of evidence and primary record, the same pattern appears again and again: the citizen is counted in every scheme, recorded in every portal, forwarded through every grievance channel, and still often not found at the point where remedy should begin.

“A citizen who cannot be remembered cannot be protected.”

This is the core question behind Era of Stupidity: Citizen Not Found. It is not an attack on technology. It is an audit of what happens when the State’s power to record grows faster than its duty to answer.

The constitutional problem

Article 12 identifies the State and the authorities whose actions must remain answerable under the Constitution of India. In the digital era, that responsibility cannot end at the door of a portal, dashboard, contractor, database, app, or automated record system.

If a public system records a citizen, denies a benefit, closes a grievance, forwards a complaint, stores personal data, certifies a public outcome, or spends public money in the citizen’s name, the constitutional question remains direct: who is responsible?

The DISHA reading

DISHA Intelligence Architecture reads this problem as an evidence and memory failure. A digital State may be able to find a person for taxation, identity, welfare enumeration, compliance, and surveillance. The test is whether the same system can find the same person for correction, hearing, explanation, appeal, compensation, and remedy.

When a government system says that a matter “does not come under grievance,” the sentence may look procedural. But if the record concerns life, dignity, money, identity, data, public health, disaster protection, or constitutional rights, the same sentence becomes a public accountability question.

Where the pattern appears

  • Digital governance expands, but citizens still struggle to locate a human officer accountable for a failed decision.
  • Data sovereignty is asserted, but records may still move through foreign infrastructure, intermediaries, apps, platforms, and vendors.
  • Sanitation and welfare success can be announced through aggregate numbers while ground records and surveys raise unresolved contradictions.
  • Disaster governance can produce plans, funds, and institutional frameworks while communities remain exposed to preventable harm.
  • Public money may be allocated, certified, reported, forwarded, or closed without a clear answer to whether the citizen received protection.

Why this matters now

The issue is not whether India should become digital. It must. The issue is whether digital governance will remain constitutional. A republic cannot treat the citizen as a data point when rights, dignity, identity, money, grievance, and remedy are at stake.

The State may know a citizen through Aadhaar, tax records, phone numbers, bank accounts, ration records, voter records, school records, hospital records, cybercrime complaints, and welfare databases. But democratic visibility is not the same as administrative traceability. A citizen is visible only when the system can answer them.
