MeitY Has 30 Days on Stolen Citizen Data — The Clock Has Run

The Supreme Court directed MeitY to examine the representation on 19 May 2026. The deadline has passed. The public record of response remains empty.

By Nitish Kumar Independent Researcher, thenitishkr Published: 20 June 2026, 09:00 IST Last verified: 20 June 2026 5 min read
Read the record Get record updates

On 19 May 2026, the Supreme Court of India routed W.P.(Crl.) No. 163/2026 to the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology as a supplementary representation, giving MeitY time to examine the petition's core claim: that personal data of Indian citizens is being held on foreign servers without legal authority, and that the State has an obligation to act. Thirty days have now elapsed. This report documents what the direction required, what the evidence record shows, and what remains publicly unresolved.

Central question Did MeitY respond within the 30-day window the Supreme Court's routing implied?

The Supreme Court's order of 19 May 2026 is not an adjudication. It is a procedural routing — MeitY was directed to examine the representation. What examination took place, and whether any response was issued, remains a matter of public record that this report tracks.

W.P.(Crl.) No. 163/2026 was filed before the Supreme Court of India, arguing that stolen personal data of Indian citizens — including biometric, KYC, and financial identity information — is being held on foreign servers by adtech infrastructure, criminal networks and data brokers. The petition asked the Court to direct its recovery or destruction and to examine the accountability gap in India's digital governance architecture.

What the Supreme Court's direction recorded

The Court on 19 May 2026 treated the petition as a supplementary representation to be examined by MeitY. This procedural step is standard: the Court routes a representation to the relevant ministry and expects a considered response. The ministry is not adjudicating the petition; it is examining whether the claims require policy action, whether existing mechanisms apply, and whether the State has a position.

What the petition asked the State to examine

The core claim in W.P.(Crl.) 163/2026 is not technical. It is constitutional. The petition argued that Indian citizens whose personal data has been extracted by adtech SDKs, stolen by criminal networks, or transferred to foreign servers without lawful basis remain without remedy. The DPDP Act 2023 provides a framework for prospective data protection. The petition asked what framework applies to data already extracted and already held abroad.

The petition is not asking the Supreme Court to recover stolen data. It is asking the State to locate its own obligation — and to state publicly what it intends to do about it.

The adtech surveillance evidence record accompanying the petition documents the architecture through which citizen data leaves Indian jurisdiction: device-level SDKs, fingerprinting, broker aggregation, and cross-border server infrastructure. The petition treats this not as a technology problem but as a constitutional governance gap — the State's digital infrastructure has made citizens legible for commercial purposes while leaving their data outside the protection of Indian law.

MeitY's existing digital governance record

The intelligence archive at W.P.(Crl.) 163/2026 and the MeitY Digital Governance Record documents MeitY's role in India's digital identity, data protection, and governance architecture. That record separates what MeitY's mandate covers from what the petition specifically raised — and what the Supreme Court's routing direction asked MeitY to examine.

MeitY's statutory responsibilities include administration of the IT Act 2000, implementation of the DPDP Act 2023, and oversight of India's digital infrastructure. The petition directly implicates each of these — the question is whether the ministry's examination acknowledged the specific constitutional claim about extraterritorial stolen data and the State's duty of response.

What the public record shows as of 20 June 2026

No MeitY order, public communication, press release or policy statement addressing W.P.(Crl.) 163/2026's core claim appears in available public records as of the date of this report. The absence of a public record does not mean no examination occurred — ministries examine representations without always issuing public communications. However, for a matter involving the constitutional rights of Indian citizens to their own personal data, the public interest in a disclosed response is clear.

Absence of a public record is itself a data point. The petition asked the State to name its obligation. Thirty days of silence does not answer that question — it defers it.

The petition's next listing before the Supreme Court will be the formal record of whether MeitY's examination produced a response the Court found adequate. Until that listing, what exists is the direction, the 30-day window, and a public record that shows the window has closed without a disclosed response.
