Evidence discipline

DISHA Claim-to-Source System

A premium DISHA archive needs every important claim tied to a source status. This page defines the working labels for future public records and corrections.

Claim labels

  • Verified record: a document, image, filing or public source that can be inspected directly.
  • Documented allegation: a claim made in a petition, grievance, complaint or authored report, without treating it as adjudicated fact.
  • DISHA assessment: the author's analysis based on linked records.
  • Unresolved question: a gap where the archive needs reply, disclosure or further verification.
  • Public commentary: opinion, interpretation or narrative context.

Source register

The current register begins in EVIDENCE-INVENTORY.md. The next product step is a public table that maps claim, source, authority, date, page number, status and correction history.

Correction rule

When a source is corrected, superseded or withdrawn, the page should keep the history visible and state what changed. The archive becomes stronger when it can show its own audit trail.

Why source labelling matters in public-interest research

Public-interest research that conflates allegation with verified fact, or that presents the researcher's own analysis as if it were an independent government record, loses credibility precisely at the moment when credibility matters most. DISHA's claim-to-source system was designed from the outset to prevent this failure. The system requires that every public claim on this site be traceable to a source label, and that the label accurately describes the epistemic status of the underlying material. This is not a bureaucratic formality; it is the mechanism by which the archive can be corrected without collapsing.

The discipline also protects against a common problem in digital-governance research: the recycling of unverified claims across platforms until they acquire the appearance of established fact through repetition. By assigning each claim a formal status — Verified record, Documented allegation, DISHA assessment or Unresolved question — the archive creates a friction point that slows the spread of premature conclusions and keeps the distinction between evidence and interpretation visible to any reader.

Verified records in practice

A verified record in the DISHA system is a document, image, filing or publicly accessible source that can be independently inspected. Examples include Supreme Court petition numbers such as W.P.(Crl.) 163/2026 and W.P.(Crl.) 394/2025, the 2013 Bihar Council of Science and Technology correspondence, and official government records that have been obtained and preserved in the evidence archive. When the archive labels something a verified record, it is asserting that the source can be located and examined. It is not asserting that the document's contents are accurate in every respect or that the underlying institution's position is correct; it is only asserting that the document exists and is accessible.

Documented allegations and DISHA assessments

A documented allegation is a claim made in a petition, complaint, grievance or authored report that the archive is preserving without treating as adjudicated fact. Many of the most important public-interest records in the DISHA archive are documented allegations: they represent what a citizen, petitioner or researcher has formally stated, and they deserve preservation precisely because the allegation is part of the public record even before any authority has responded. A DISHA assessment, by contrast, is the author's own analysis based on linked source records. It is distinguished from a verified record because it involves interpretive judgement, and from a documented allegation because it is not simply reporting what another party has claimed. It is labelled explicitly so that readers understand they are reading Nitish Kumar's reasoning, not a primary source document.

Unresolved questions as a research output

An unresolved question is not a failure of the archive; it is one of its most important outputs. When the evidence inventory identifies a gap — an unanswered government response, a missing disclosure, a question about the origin or custodian of a data set — publishing that gap as an unresolved question serves the public interest by putting the question on the record. It invites institutional response, correction or additional sourcing. The archive currently holds several unresolved questions concerning data sovereignty and MeitY records that form part of the Supreme Court petition W.P.(Crl.) 163/2026.
